Five traits. One shape.

How to get an honest result

  • Answer for your typical week, not your best day or your worst.
  • Go with your first instinct. Overthinking a statement rarely improves your answer.
  • Describe who you are, not who you're trying to become. The test can't help the aspirational version of you.
  • Use "Neither" only when you truly can't decide. A test full of middle answers tells you nothing.
  • Skip the test when you're exhausted or upset. Your mood leaks into your answers.

Quick answers

Why not the 16 personality types?

The four-letter type tests sort a smooth range into two boxes per letter. Someone near the middle lands on one side or the other, and studies of the popular type tests find many people get a different type when they retake them. The Big Five keeps the whole range, so you see how far you lean, not just which way.

Will my scores change if I retake it?

Expect small shifts. That's why every trait shows a likely range next to the score.

Where do my answers go?

They stay in your browser. The page has no server and no sign-up. The only time data leaves your device is when you send a friend a link, and then only the ratings in that link.

Where do the questions come from?

They come from the International Personality Item Pool, a public-domain library built by researchers. This test uses 87 questions from the IPIP-NEO-120. Full sources sit at the bottom of your results.

How much to trust this

Your five trait scores are solid enough for self-reflection. The research team behind these questions says they work for research but should not drive big decisions about a person, like hiring. Treat the sub-trait scores as rougher still, since each one rests on three questions.

The shaded band on each trait shows a likely range. Take the test again in a different mood and you'll land somewhere in it.

The comparison group is 320,000 mostly American volunteers who chose to take an online personality test. People like that tend to score higher on curiosity and empathy than the general public, so your Openness percentile may read a little low.

You also see yourself from the inside. In validation studies, friends' ratings matched people's self-ratings best on Openness and worst on Agreeableness. Ask someone close to you which scores surprise them.

Methods and sources

Questions come from the IPIP-NEO-120 (Johnson, 2014), a public-domain inventory from the International Personality Item Pool. This version keeps the three strongest questions per sub-trait, ranked by each question's correlation with its scale in the original paper, for 87 questions total. It drops the political-values sub-trait because it asks about voting, which varies by country and loads weakly on Openness. Percentiles use norms from Kajonius & Johnson (2019), rescaled from four to three questions, so treat them as close estimates.
